2.1. Parts of Speech Noun, Pronoun, Verb, Adjective, Adverb and Interjection- Meaning and Examples (Recapitulation) Prepositions- In, into, On, At, for, Since, between, among, to, towards Connectors - If, Unless, Otherwise, Because, Therefore, Who, Which, Where, When, Why.

2.1.1. Nouns

Noun: A noun is a word used to name a person, place, thing, or idea. Nouns can be singular or plural.

  • Example:

> Example: The book is on the table. (Singular noun)

> Example: The books are in the bag. (Plural noun)

2.1.2. Pronouns

Pronoun: A pronoun is a word that takes the place of a noun. Pronouns are used to avoid repetition of nouns and to make writing more concise.

  • Example:

> Example: She read the book. (Pronoun)

> Example: We will go to the park. (Pronoun)

2.1.3. Verbs

Verb: A verb is a word that expresses an action, occurrence, or state of being. Verbs are the main part of a sentence that describe what the subject is doing.

  • Example:

> Example: She reads the book. (Verb: reads)

> Example: The book is on the table. (Verb: is)

2.1.4. Adjectives

Adjective: An adjective is a word that modifies a noun or a pronoun. Adjectives describe or give more information about the noun or pronoun.

  • Example:

> Example: The big book is on the table. (Adjective: big)

> Example: She read the interesting book. (Adjective: interesting)

2.1.5. Adverbs

Adverb: An adverb is a word that modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. Adverbs usually express manner, place, time, or frequency.

  • Example:

> Example: She reads the book quickly. (Adverb: quickly)

> Example: The book is on the table. (Adverb: on)

2.1.6. Interjections

Interjection: An interjection is a word used to express sudden feelings or emotions. Interjections are often followed by an exclamation mark.

  • Example:

> Example: Ouch! That hurt. (Interjection: Ouch)

2.1.7. Prepositions

Preposition: A preposition is a word that shows the relationship between a noun or pronoun and other words in a sentence. Common prepositions include in, into, on, at, for, since, between, among, to, towards.

  • Example:

> Example: The book is in the bag. (Preposition: in)

> Example: He went to the store. (Preposition: to)

2.1.8. Connectors

Connectors: Connectors are words that link parts of a sentence or connect sentences. Common connectors include if, unless, otherwise, because, therefore, who, which, where, when, why.

  • Example:

> Example: If it rains, we will stay inside. (Connector: If)

> Example: Because it rained, we stayed inside. (Connector: Because)

> Example: We will go to the park, where we can play. (Connector: Where)

2.1.9. Sentence Patterns and Word Order

Identifying and using basic sentence patterns correctly is essential for proper communication. A typical sentence pattern includes a subject, verb, and object. Understanding and using correct word order is crucial.

  • Example:

> Example: The teacher gave the students books. (Subject: The teacher, Verb: gave, Object: the students, Object complement: books)

2.2. Tenses

Tenses are used to indicate the time of an action or state. English has three main tenses: simple present, simple past, and simple future.

2.2.1. Simple Present Tense

The simple present tense is used to describe habitual actions, general truths, or future timetables.

  • Example:

> Example: She reads the book every day. (Simple present for habitual actions)

> Example: The book is on the table. (Simple present for general truths)

2.2.2. Simple Past Tense

The simple past tense is used to describe actions that happened in the past.

  • Example:

> Example: She read the book yesterday. (Simple past for actions in the past)

2.2.3. Simple Future Tense

The simple future tense is used to describe actions that will happen in the future.

  • Example:

> Example: She will read the book tomorrow. (Simple future for future actions)

2.2.4. Practice Example

Example
  • Given: She reads the book every day.
  • Rewrite in the past tense: She read the book every day.
  • Rewrite in the future tense: She will read the book every day.

2.2.1. Present Tense (Simple, Continuous, Perfect, Perfect Continuous)

2.2.1.1. Present Simple Tense

The Present Simple Tense is used to describe actions that are habitual, general, or true at the time of speaking. It is also used for scheduled or fixed events. The structure of the Present Simple Tense is:

  • Positive: Subject + Base form of verb
  • Negative: Subject + do/does + not + Base form of verb
  • Question: Do/Does + Subject + Base form of verb

Example:

Example


- Positive: I drink coffee every morning.
- Negative: I do not drink coffee every morning.
- Question: Do you drink coffee every morning?

2.2.1.2. Present Continuous Tense

The Present Continuous Tense is used to describe actions that are happening now or in the immediate future. It is formed using the auxiliary verb "am/is/are" followed by the base form of the verb plus -ing.

  • Positive: Subject + am/is/are + verb + -ing
  • Negative: Subject + am/is/are + not + verb + -ing
  • Question: Am/I/Is/Are + subject + verb + -ing

Example:

Example


- Positive: She is reading a book.
- Negative: She is not reading a book.
- Question: Are you reading a book?

2.2.1.3. Present Perfect Tense

The Present Perfect Tense is used to describe actions that started in the past and have a connection to the present. It is formed using "have/has" followed by the past participle of the verb.

  • Positive: Subject + have/has + past participle
  • Negative: Subject + have/has + not + past participle
  • Question: Have/Has + subject + past participle

Example:

Example


- Positive: I have read that book.
- Negative: I have not read that book.
- Question: Have you read that book?

2.2.1.4. Present Perfect Continuous Tense

The Present Perfect Continuous Tense is used to describe actions that started in the past and continue up to the present. It is formed using "have/has" followed by the present participle of the verb.

  • Positive: Subject + have/has + been + verb + -ing
  • Negative: Subject + have/has + not + been + verb + -ing
  • Question: Have/Has + subject + been + verb + -ing

Example:

Example


- Positive: She has been writing letters all day.
- Negative: She has not been writing letters all day.
- Question: Has she been writing letters all day?

2.2.2. Past Tense (Simple, Continuous, Perfect)

2.2.2.1. Past Simple Tense

The Past Simple Tense is used to describe completed actions in the past. It is formed using the base form of the verb for regular verbs and the appropriate past form for irregular verbs.

  • Positive: Subject + past form of verb
  • Negative: Subject + did + not + Base form of verb
  • Question: Did + subject + Base form of verb

Example:

Example


- Positive: She drank coffee yesterday.
- Negative: She did not drink coffee yesterday.
- Question: Did she drink coffee yesterday?

2.2.2.2. Past Continuous Tense

The Past Continuous Tense is used to describe actions that were in progress in the past. It is formed using the auxiliary verb "was/were" followed by the base form of the verb plus -ing.

  • Positive: Subject + was/were + verb + -ing
  • Negative: Subject + was/were + not + verb + -ing
  • Question: Was/Were + subject + verb + -ing

Example:

Example


- Positive: She was reading a book yesterday.
- Negative: She was not reading a book yesterday.
- Question: Was she reading a book yesterday?

2.2.2.3. Past Perfect Tense

The Past Perfect Tense is used to describe actions that were completed before another past action. It is formed using "had" followed by the past participle of the verb.

  • Positive: Subject + had + past participle
  • Negative: Subject + had + not + past participle
  • Question: Had + subject + past participle

Example:

Example


- Positive: She had read that book before the meeting.
- Negative: She had not read that book before the meeting.
- Question: Had she read that book before the meeting?

2.2.2.4. Past Perfect Continuous Tense

The Past Perfect Continuous Tense is used to describe actions that started in the past and continued up to another past time. It is formed using "had" followed by the present participle of the verb.

  • Positive: Subject + had + been + verb + -ing
  • Negative: Subject + had + not + been + verb + -ing
  • Question: Had + subject + been + verb + -ing

Example:

Example


- Positive: She had been writing letters all day before the meeting.
- Negative: She had not been writing letters all day before the meeting.
- Question: Had she been writing letters all day before the meeting?

2.2.3. Future Tense (Simple)

Definition and Usage

Future Tense (Simple) is used to express actions or events that will occur at a future time. It is formed using the verb "will" followed by the base form of the main verb. This tense is often used to make predictions, give opinions, and express intentions.

Formation

  • Positive Statements:
  • Subject + will + base form of verb + (object)
  • Example: I will go to the market tomorrow.
  • Negative Statements:
  • Subject + will + not + base form of verb + (object)
  • Example: She will not attend the meeting today.
  • Question Form:
  • Will + subject + base form of verb + (object) + ?
  • Example: Will they come to the party tonight?

Examples

Example

- Positive: They will visit their relatives next week.
- Negative: We will not cancel the project.
- Question: Will he come to the meeting?

Practical Application

The future tense is commonly used in day-to-day communication to plan and make decisions. For example, if a student is planning their weekend, they might say:

Example

- I will study for two hours this evening and then go for a walk.
- They will start their project next Monday.

Worked Example

Example

- Question: What will you do tomorrow morning?
- Answer: I will attend a workshop on sustainable engineering practices.

Importance in Sentence Formation

Future tense is essential for expressing future events or intentions. It helps in making clear and precise statements about what is planned or predicted to happen in the future. For instance, when discussing upcoming projects or events, the use of future tense ensures that the timing is correctly conveyed.

2.3. Modal Auxiliaries (Can, Could, May, Might, Shall, Should, Will, Would, Must, Have to, Ought to)

Definition and Usage

Modal Auxiliaries are words used to express possibility, permission, obligation, and advice. These words modify the meaning of the main verb and are used to convey different shades of meaning. Here, we will cover the common modal auxiliaries and their usage in communication skills.

Detailed Explanation of Each Modal

  • Can (Present Ability):
  • Definition: Used to express ability or capability in the present.
  • Example: She can speak multiple languages.
  • Could (Past Ability):
  • Definition: Used to express ability or capability in the past.
  • Example: He could ride a bicycle when he was five.
  • May (Permission):
  • Definition: Used to ask for or give permission.
  • Example: May I use your calculator?
  • Might (Possibility):
  • Definition: Used to express a possibility or a chance.
  • Example: It might rain tomorrow.
  • Shall (Future Ought):
  • Definition: Used to express a future intention or obligation.
  • Example: We shall meet at the library this afternoon.
  • Should (Advice):
  • Definition: Used to give advice or make suggestions.
  • Example: You should start your project early.
  • Will (Future Intent):
  • Definition: Used to express a future intention or a promise.
  • Example: She will attend the workshop tomorrow.
  • Would (Past Habit):
  • Definition: Used to express a past habit or willingness.
  • Example: He would help his sister with homework.
  • Must (Obligation):
  • Definition: Used to express a strong obligation or necessity.
  • Example: You must submit your report by Friday.
  • Have to (Obligation):
  • Definition: Used to express a present or future obligation.
  • Example: We have to finish the assignment today.
  • Ought to (Ought):
  • Definition: Used to express what one thinks is right or wrong.
  • Example: They ought to arrive on time.

Practical Application

Modal auxiliaries are crucial in communication as they help to convey the speaker's intentions, abilities, and obligations clearly. For example, in a professional setting, when giving instructions, the use of modal auxiliaries can make the instructions more clear and precise.

Modal auxiliaries are essential for expressing various shades of meaning in English. They help to convey the speaker's intentions, capabilities, and obligations clearly. Using the correct modal auxiliary can make the communication more precise and effective.

2.4. Subject- Verb Agreement

Subject-verb agreement is a fundamental aspect of English grammar. It involves ensuring that the subject of a sentence agrees with the verb in terms of number (singular or plural). This agreement is crucial for clear communication and correct sentence construction. In this section, we will explore the rules and common pitfalls of subject-verb agreement.

2.4.1. Singular and Plural Subjects

  • Singular Subject: A singular subject is a noun or pronoun that refers to one person, place, thing, or idea. It requires a singular verb.
  • Plural Subject: A plural subject is a noun or pronoun that refers to more than one person, place, thing, or idea. It requires a plural verb.

Example:

Example

- The cat (singular subject) sleeps (singular verb).
- The cats (plural subject) sleep (plural verb).

2.4.2. Collective Nouns

Collective nouns are nouns that refer to a group of people or things. They can be treated as singular or plural depending on the context.

  • Singular Treatment: When the collective noun is treated as a single unit, the verb should be singular.
  • Plural Treatment: When the collective noun is treated as individual members, the verb should be plural.

Example:

Example

- The team (singular treatment) is (singular verb) playing (singular verb).
- The team (plural treatment) are (plural verb) playing (plural verb).

2.4.3. Verbs with Singular and Plural Subjects

Some verbs can take either a singular or plural subject, depending on the context.

  • Singular Subject:
  • Each, every, everyone, anybody, someone, nobody, anything, something, nothing, nobody, everybody, anyone, someone.
  • Plural Subject:
  • Several, many, few, both, all, these, those, they.

Example:

Example

- Each student (singular subject) has (singular verb) their own notebook (singular object).
- Several students (plural subject) have (plural verb) their own notebooks (plural object).

2.4.4. Agreement with Indefinite Pronouns

Indefinite pronouns can be tricky in subject-verb agreement.

  • Singular Indefinite Pronouns: each, everyone, everybody, anyone, nobody, someone, no one, either, neither, one.
  • Plural Indefinite Pronouns: many, few, both, all, these, those, they.

Example:

Example

- Each student (singular indefinite pronoun) has (singular verb) their own notebook (singular object).
- Both of them (plural indefinite pronoun) have (plural verb) their own notebooks (plural object).

2.4.5. Agreement with Noun Clauses

Noun clauses can act as subjects and require the appropriate verb form.

  • Singular Noun Clause:
  • That, what, who, which.
  • Plural Noun Clause:
  • All, both, few, many, most, none, some.

Example:

Example

- What you said (singular noun clause) is (singular verb) correct (singular adjective).
- All that is needed (plural noun clause) are (plural verb) available (plural adjective).

2.5. Basic Sentence Patterns of English (Explanation of S, V, O, A, C) S-Subject, V-Verb, O- Object, A-Adverbial and C- Complement

The four basic sentence patterns of English are essential for constructing clear and effective sentences. Each pattern follows a specific structure involving the subject (S), verb (V), object (O), adverbial (A), and complement (C).

2.5.1. S + V

This pattern is the simplest and most common sentence structure. It involves a subject performing an action.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) teaches (V).

2.5.2. S + V + O

In this pattern, the subject performs an action on the object.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) teaches (V) students (O).

2.5.3. S + V + A

Here, the subject performs an action, and the adverb modifies the verb to describe how the action is performed.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) teaches (V) enthusiastically (A).

2.5.4. S + V + C

In this pattern, the subject performs an action, and the complement provides additional information about the subject.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) is (V) knowledgeable (C).

2.5.5. S + V + O + A

This pattern combines the previous two patterns, adding an adverb to modify the verb.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) teaches (V) students (O) enthusiastically (A).

2.5.6. S + V + O + C

This pattern combines the previous two patterns, adding a complement to provide additional information about the object.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) teaches (V) students (O) knowledge (C).

2.5.7. S + V + A + C

This pattern combines the previous two patterns, adding an adverb to modify the verb and a complement to provide additional information about the subject.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) teaches (V) enthusiastically (A) knowledge (C).

2.5.8. S + V + O + A + C

This is the most complex pattern, combining all the elements.

Example:

Example

- The teacher (S) teaches (V) students (O) enthusiastically (A) knowledge (C).

By understanding and applying these sentence patterns, you can construct clear and grammatically correct sentences in English.
